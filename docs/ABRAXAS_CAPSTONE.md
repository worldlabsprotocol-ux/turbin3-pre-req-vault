## Concept overview (2–5 sentences)

*Source: founder-provided positioning (Oct 2026). Review and edit in your voice before treating as final.*

Abraxas is a wallet-native trust and verification protocol aimed at institutions, real-world assets, and tokenized markets. Tokenization creates digital representation; Abraxas focuses on **verification**—whether a specific action or claim can be trusted under explicit rules. Users keep control of their wallets and evidence; the protocol checks narrowly defined claims and authorization conditions using cryptographic proofs, issuer-backed evidence where required, signed decisions, and results that others can verify independently. It is trust infrastructure, not a conventional SaaS product with blockchain added on—designed to limit unnecessary identity collection while still supporting strong authorization, replay protection, revocation, and tenant isolation.

## Value proposition & product-market fit (one paragraph)

*Founder-authored.*

I've been involved in DeFi and Web3 since 2017, watching the industry push toward mainstream adoption. For years, we expected retail users to drive that transition. Instead, institutional adoption started moving the industry forward through a different avenue.

That's progress, but it also creates a tension. Institutions naturally want to control the rails, much like traditional banks and centralized exchanges. Meanwhile, decentralization, which made this industry interesting in the first place, risks taking a backseat.

Abraxas is being built around that gap. Institutions need reliable verification, authorization, and compliance infrastructure, but users shouldn't have to surrender unnecessary personal information every time they need to prove something. Abraxas provides a wallet-native trust layer where evidence can be verified for a specific action without making identity collection the default.

## Founder–market fit (a few sentences)

*Founder-authored.*

My perspective comes from years of participating in DeFi, navigating decentralized protocols, and watching institutional adoption reshape Web3. Building in the U.S. also means understanding how regulatory uncertainty has influenced the industry and the infrastructure people choose to build.

Through that experience, I've consistently valued protocols that preserve user control rather than simply recreating centralized financial systems onchain.

Now I'm applying that perspective directly through Abraxas. I've moved beyond the concept stage into building wallet-based authorization, credential verification, and independently verifiable trust decisions.

I understand both why institutions need dependable infrastructure and why users need protection from unnecessary data exposure. I believe the next phase of adoption should deliver both.
